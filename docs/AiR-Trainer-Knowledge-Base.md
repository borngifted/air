# AiR Trainer Knowledge Base

## Purpose of this handbook

This handbook is the **trainer-facing operating system** for AiR. It is separate from the learner site. Use it to prepare facilitators, run workshops, adapt the curriculum for learners beginning at age eight, coach mixed-age groups, and understand the educational reasoning behind every exercise.

AiR is not a tour of software. The purpose is to build a durable mindset that remains useful as tools change:

> **Choose a human outcome. Give useful direction. Challenge the result. Put the work in the world.**

The learner should leave every session with evidence of action: a mission, brief, marked-up answer, revised artifact, repeatable workflow, or real human response. Completion is not measured by vocabulary recall alone.

## 1. The trainer promise

An AiR trainer makes complex work feel possible without pretending it is simple. The trainer reduces cognitive load, not intellectual honesty. This means using familiar stories, introducing one idea at a time, making invisible decisions visible, and offering enough support for a first move.

| Trainer commitment | What it looks like in practice |
|---|---|
| Start with people | Ask who should benefit before naming a tool |
| Use plain language | Say “pattern-based guess” before introducing technical vocabulary |
| Make one move visible | Replace long instructions with one action, one example, and one check |
| Keep judgment human | Treat output as a draft that requires review and ownership |
| Match review to consequence | Increase evidence, expertise, and human control as potential harm increases |
| Protect privacy | Never ask learners to expose personal details to demonstrate a concept |
| Finish the loop | Put a useful artifact in front of a real person and learn from the response |

## 2. The AiR learning architecture

The curriculum contains four learning paths. In the platform, each path is the canonical course structure, with a module, three lessons, interactive checkpoints, and Explore/Create/Build exercises.

| Path | Core question | Learner evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Clear** | What human change matters? | A one-sentence mission and an accurate explanation of AI as prediction |
| **Direct** | What does the system need to know and do? | A brief with audience, purpose, context, boundaries, and success criteria |
| **Judge** | What could be wrong, harmful, unfair, private, or unfit? | A documented review and a human decision |
| **Make** | What can we finish, repeat, release, and improve? | A useful artifact, workflow, and real-world response |

### The five-beat lesson

Every lesson uses the same rhythm. Learners should become familiar with the rhythm so they can focus on the new idea.

1. **See it.** Begin with a familiar story, object, situation, or demonstration.
2. **Name it.** State one big idea in plain language.
3. **Try it.** Give one small action with a worked example.
4. **Check it.** Ask what could be wrong, missing, harmful, inaccessible, or private.
5. **Share it.** Invite the learner to explain what changed and choose a next move.

### Three entry modes

**Explore** is not a remedial level. It uses play, drawing, speaking, sorting, movement, and strong scaffolding. It is effective for children, first-time learners, language learners, and anyone who needs to reduce screen or reading demands.

**Create** applies the concept to a real project and audience. It is the default route for most independent learners.

**Build** adds systems, workflows, production constraints, automation, governance, and higher-consequence decision-making. It is not an age badge; a young learner can choose Build when ready.

## 3. Preparing to facilitate

### Before the session

Review the lesson promise and identify the single idea learners must carry away. Choose one story that fits their context. Test any demonstration. Prepare a no-device version. Decide what information learners must not enter into an AI tool. Identify the consequence level of the exercise and the required human review.

| Preparation question | Minimum acceptable answer |
|---|---|
| Who is in the room? | Approximate ages, reading needs, languages, access needs, AI experience |
| What can they make today? | A small artifact that can be completed or tested in the session |
| What may not be shared? | Personal, school, client, health, financial, legal, account, or confidential data |
| What requires verification? | Every factual claim that may affect a decision |
| What remains human-led? | Purpose, consequence judgment, approval, and release |
| How can learners participate without a device? | Paper, movement, partner talk, facilitator demonstration, or printed cards |

### Recommended session formats

| Format | Time | Flow |
|---|---:|---|
| Quick activation | 20 minutes | See it, Name it, one Explore activity, one reflection |
| Core lesson | 45–60 minutes | Five-beat lesson plus one selected exercise |
| Studio workshop | 90 minutes | Lesson, Create or Build work period, peer review, release plan |
| Family session | 60 minutes | Shared story, mixed-age pairs, no-device activity, family project |
| Team lab | 2–3 hours | Two connected lessons, real workflow application, risk review, owner assignment |

## 4. Facilitation language

Use language that keeps learners active and preserves uncertainty.

| Instead of | Say |
|---|---|
| “The AI knows…” | “The system predicts…” or “The output suggests…” |
| “Write the perfect prompt.” | “Give the next useful piece of direction.” |
| “That answer is wrong.” | “Which claim needs evidence?” |
| “Use the best tool.” | “Choose the tool that fits the mission and consequence.” |
| “Make it professional.” | “What should the audience understand, feel, or do?” |
| “You finished the content.” | “What can you now do, inspect, or release?” |
| “This is easy.” | “We will take one clear move at a time.” |

Never shame a learner for using AI, avoiding AI, believing a fluent answer, needing reading support, or choosing the Explore mode. AiR builds agency through practice, not fear.

## 5. Child-centered participation and safeguarding

AiR can be learned beginning at age eight, but the platform is not a substitute for appropriate adult supervision, local safeguarding policy, or parental consent where required. Trainers must follow the rules of their school, organization, and jurisdiction.

### Community privacy script

Read this before any public or group sharing activity:

> Use a short display name. Do not share your full name, school, address, phone number, daily schedule, password, private images, or information that belongs to someone else. Share what you tried and learned—not private details.

Private lesson reflections should remain private unless a learner deliberately creates a separate community post. AiR does not use direct messaging, public age fields, school fields, location fields, follower counts, or public progress rankings.

### Constructive response protocol

Teach learners to respond with three sentence starters:

1. **I noticed…** Describe the work rather than judging the person.
2. **I wondered…** Ask a genuine question rather than assuming intent.
3. **One idea to try…** Offer a specific experiment, not a command.

If a post contains concerning personal information, harmful content, or a disclosure that requires adult action, do not investigate in public. Preserve dignity, use the reporting workflow, and follow the responsible organization’s safeguarding procedure.

## 6. Accessibility and mixed-age delivery

Read essential prompts aloud. Provide printed copies at a readable size. Allow speaking, drawing, typing, pointing, sorting, and partner dictation as valid responses. Avoid requiring fast reading or public performance. Describe visual examples. Caption lesson videos and provide transcripts. Use color plus labels, not color alone.

In mixed-age pairs, do not automatically make the older learner the expert. Give reciprocal roles. A younger learner can be the **clarity checker** who notices jargon and hidden assumptions. An experienced learner can be the **consequence checker** who identifies risks, dependencies, and production constraints.

## 7. Lesson facilitation guides

### Lesson 01.1 — Clear the Air

**Learner promise:** Write one sentence that explains why AI might be useful without naming a tool.

**Age-eight explanation:** A tool is like something in a toolbox. Before choosing a tool, decide what you want to fix or make and who it should help.

**Opening demonstration:** Place several tool cards or objects on a table. Ask learners to choose the best one without telling them the task. After disagreement, reveal a task such as attaching two papers or measuring a line. Discuss why purpose comes first.

| Element | Guidance |
|---|---|
| Explore | Say or draw one person and one helpful change; remove every tool name |
| Create | List five AI ambitions, cross out borrowed goals, and rewrite the strongest survivor |
| Build | Rank three missions by real demand, observable change, and consequence |
| Common misconception | “My mission is to use AI.” Ask what should be different for a person afterward |
| Evidence | “I want to make ___ so that ___ can ___.” |
| Discussion | What did you cross out, and what was still worth carrying? |

### Lesson 01.2 — Machines Make Guesses

**Learner promise:** Explain AI using the words **pattern**, **guess**, and **check**.

**Age-eight explanation:** AI looks at many examples and guesses what fits next. A good guess can sound sure and still be wrong.

**Opening demonstration:** Complete familiar patterns aloud. Then show a pattern with a surprising final item. Ask whether the common guess was foolish. Explain that it was reasonable but not true.

| Element | Guidance |
|---|---|
| Explore | Play “finish the pattern,” including one surprise ending |
| Create | Highlight claims in a generated answer and mark which have evidence |
| Build | Give the same task three different context blocks and compare inventions |
| Common misconception | “It sounds confident, so it knows.” Separate tone from evidence |
| Evidence | A two-sentence, non-magical explanation of AI |
| Discussion | When could a confident guess create a real problem? |

### Lesson 01.3 — Choose One Mission

**Learner promise:** Choose a person, a present problem, and an observable change.

**Age-eight explanation:** A flashlight helps you see one part of a dark room. A clear mission helps everyone aim at one useful change.

**Opening demonstration:** Compare “make school better” with “help one new student find lunch on their first day.” Ask which mission shows a next action.

| Element | Guidance |
|---|---|
| Explore | Create a fictional person card without private real-world details |
| Create | Interview one real person, then revise the mission after listening |
| Build | Define before state, after state, and one success signal |
| Common misconception | “Specific means less ambitious.” Show how tested specifics can scale |
| Evidence | Person + problem + change + success signal |
| Discussion | Whose day would be different if this worked? |

### Lesson 02.1 — Be the Director

**Learner promise:** Turn “make this better” into a usable creative brief.

**Age-eight explanation:** A director helps everyone see the same scene. AI cannot see the picture in your head unless you describe the job and what good means.

**Opening demonstration:** Ask two volunteers to draw from “make a cool robot,” then repeat with audience, feeling, details, and constraints. Compare variation, not artistic quality.

| Element | Guidance |
|---|---|
| Explore | Direct a drawing that another person cannot see |
| Create | Write audience, desired action, tone, must-include content, and example |
| Build | Create five acceptance criteria another reviewer can apply |
| Common misconception | “More adjectives make better direction.” Ask for observable criteria |
| Evidence | A brief another person could act on without guessing the core goal |
| Discussion | Which human decision improved the direction most? |

### Lesson 02.2 — Give Useful Context

**Learner promise:** Supply information that changes the answer while protecting private details.

**Age-eight explanation:** “What should I wear?” needs a place, weather, and activity. It never needs your password or home address.

**Opening demonstration:** Ask the same question with three different situation cards. Identify which details changed the useful answer.

| Element | Guidance |
|---|---|
| Explore | Change one detail and compare the resulting advice |
| Create | Sort ten details into Keep, Skip, and Protect |
| Build | Build a reusable context template with privacy exclusions |
| Common misconception | “More data is always better.” Contrast relevance with volume |
| Evidence | Three useful context facts and one protected detail |
| Discussion | What changed the result most, and what stayed private? |

### Lesson 02.3 — Ask for Options

**Learner promise:** Generate meaningfully different directions and explain a human choice.

**Age-eight explanation:** The first team name is not automatically the best one. Several different ideas help you notice what you actually like and need.

**Opening demonstration:** Ask for three solutions that are safe, playful, and surprising. Reject outputs that only change wording.

| Element | Guidance |
|---|---|
| Explore | Make three options using three clearly different styles |
| Create | Compare options for fit, clarity, effort, and risk |
| Build | Define strategies before generation and document each tradeoff |
| Common misconception | “More options always help.” Keep the set small and meaningfully diverse |
| Evidence | A comparison and a reasoned choice or combination |
| Discussion | What did a rejected option teach you? |

### Lesson 03.1 — Spot the Guess

**Learner promise:** Identify checkable claims and connect each one to a source.

**Age-eight explanation:** A detective looks for clues. Dates, numbers, names, quotes, and very specific facts need clues you can inspect.

**Opening demonstration:** Read two equally confident statements, one sourced and one invented. Ask what confidence alone proves.

| Element | Guidance |
|---|---|
| Explore | Underline facts, circle opinions, and question unsupported specifics |
| Create | Build a claim, source, result, and action ledger |
| Build | List failure hypotheses and search for disconfirming evidence |
| Common misconception | “Good spelling means good facts.” Use a polished false example |
| Evidence | A marked-up output with sources and revisions |
| Discussion | Which detail looked trustworthy until you checked it? |

### Lesson 03.2 — Check the Stakes

**Learner promise:** Match review and human control to the consequence of error.

**Age-eight explanation:** A wrong crayon color and a wrong medicine instruction are not the same kind of mistake.

**Opening demonstration:** Mark four areas: Practice, Verify, Pause, and Human-Led. Read scenarios and invite learners to move to a zone, then explain.

| Element | Guidance |
|---|---|
| Explore | Place everyday tasks on a consequence ladder |
| Create | Name possible harm, affected people, sources, reviewer, and stop condition |
| Build | Define allowed support, prohibited decisions, escalation, and accountable owner |
| Common misconception | “Human review means glancing at it.” Define expertise and evidence needed |
| Evidence | A written review plan proportional to consequence |
| Discussion | What belongs in the human-led zone, and why? |

### Lesson 03.3 — Make It Fit People

**Learner promise:** Review an artifact for clarity, accessibility, representation, privacy, and tone.

**Age-eight explanation:** Correct words can still fail if someone cannot read, reach, understand, trust, or safely use them.

**Opening demonstration:** Show a correct instruction in tiny type, unfamiliar language, or low contrast. Ask whether correct equals useful.

| Element | Guidance |
|---|---|
| Explore | Look at one message from a different person’s situation |
| Create | Run five human-fit checks and revise the weakest area |
| Build | Write inclusive acceptance tests for access, bandwidth, privacy, and representation |
| Common misconception | “Accessibility is a final design check.” Make it part of the first brief |
| Evidence | A before-and-after revision tied to an audience need |
| Discussion | What assumption became visible when you changed perspective? |

### Lesson 04.1 — Make a First Version

**Learner promise:** Define and finish the smallest version that can create useful evidence.

**Age-eight explanation:** A paper airplane must leave the desk before you can see how it flies.

**Opening demonstration:** Compare a large plan with a small paper prototype. Ask which one a person can react to today.

| Element | Guidance |
|---|---|
| Explore | Make the idea using paper, blocks, or three sentences |
| Create | Keep only the features required for the first useful outcome |
| Build | Prototype the assumption most likely to invalidate the mission |
| Common misconception | “First version means careless.” It should be small, safe, and testable |
| Evidence | A real artifact and one thing learned by using it |
| Discussion | What did the first version teach that planning could not? |

### Lesson 04.2 — Build Your Way

**Learner promise:** Turn one useful result into a repeatable process with a human decision point.

**Age-eight explanation:** A recipe names steps and checks. “Make good soup” is a wish, not a process.

**Opening demonstration:** Give incomplete sandwich instructions and follow them literally. Invite learners to repair missing steps and decisions.

| Element | Guidance |
|---|---|
| Explore | Teach a five-step recipe and mark where a partner gets stuck |
| Create | Document trigger, input, generation, review, revision, and delivery |
| Build | Add approvals, logs, fallbacks, privacy limits, and accountable owner |
| Common misconception | “Automation removes responsibility.” Name the human owner explicitly |
| Evidence | A workflow another person can repeat and check |
| Discussion | Which step must stay a human decision? |

### Lesson 04.3 — Put It in the World

**Learner promise:** Deliver one artifact, gather one focused response, and choose the next improvement.

**Age-eight explanation:** Work cannot help someone while it stays hidden. Sharing lets the real world teach you.

**Opening demonstration:** Throw a paper airplane, observe what happens, change one fold, and throw again. Connect feedback to iteration rather than personal worth.

| Element | Guidance |
|---|---|
| Explore | Show one trusted person and ask what they think the work is for |
| Create | Check facts, access, credits, privacy, and mobile use before release |
| Build | Document version, dependencies, monitoring, rollback, feedback, and owner |
| Common misconception | “Release means public to everyone.” One intended user can complete the loop |
| Evidence | Delivery, response, reflection, and next revision |
| Discussion | What did a real person notice, and what changes next? |

## 8. Assessment without grades

AiR uses **evidence of practice**, not public scores or leaderboards. A trainer can assess growth with four questions.

| Dimension | Beginning | Developing | Ready to apply |
|---|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Names a tool or vague ambition | Names a person or problem | Connects person, problem, change, and success signal |
| Direction | Gives a generic command | Adds some audience or context | Provides purpose, context, boundaries, criteria, and unknowns |
| Judgment | Accepts or rejects by feeling | Checks selected claims | Calibrates evidence and human control to consequence |
| Release | Keeps work private or unfinished | Shares a draft without a focused question | Delivers to the intended person, gathers evidence, and chooses a revision |

Use the rubric to plan feedback, not label identity. Say, “This brief needs a clearer audience,” not, “You are a beginner.”

## 9. Trainer observation notes

After every session, record:

1. Which story made the idea visible?
2. Where did instructions become too dense?
3. Which misconception appeared?
4. Who participated less, and what access change may help?
5. What evidence showed transfer beyond the example?
6. What private or high-consequence information almost entered the activity?
7. What will you change before the next delivery?

## 11. Trainer readiness checklist

Before leading AiR independently, a trainer should be able to:

- Explain AI as pattern-based prediction without relying on magical or human-like language.
- Facilitate all five lesson beats in forty-five minutes.
- Offer Explore, Create, and Build routes without ranking learners.
- Identify private information and stop it from entering a tool or community post.
- Distinguish low-, medium-, and high-consequence tasks.
- Turn a vague prompt into a brief with audience, purpose, context, boundaries, and checks.
- Demonstrate how to verify a claim with an appropriate source.
- Facilitate constructive feedback using “I noticed, I wondered, one idea to try.”
- Provide a no-device and read-aloud alternative.
- Help a learner release a small artifact to an intended person and gather a useful response.
- Record delivery observations and improve the next session.
