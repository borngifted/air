# AiR Curriculum Research Notes

## Framework findings

UNESCO’s **AI Competency Framework for Students** defines twelve competencies across four dimensions: a human-centred mindset, ethics of AI, AI techniques and applications, and AI system design. It uses three progression levels—Understand, Apply, and Create—and emphasizes critical judgment, responsible citizenship, foundational AI knowledge, inclusion, and sustainability.[1]

NIST’s **AI Risk Management Framework** is designed to improve the trustworthiness of AI while supporting innovation and mitigating risk.[2] For AiR, this translates into a practical learner rule: the higher the consequence of a task, the more deliberate the human review, verification, privacy protection, and decision authority must be.

Stanford Teaching Commons organizes generative-AI literacy into four overlapping domains: functional, ethical, rhetorical, and pedagogical literacy. The model moves learners from foundational awareness to applied understanding and finally to creating new knowledge and contributing positively to a learning community.[3]

TeachAI describes AI literacy as the combination of technical knowledge, durable skills, and future-ready attitudes needed to engage with, create with, manage, and design AI while critically evaluating its benefits, risks, and ethical implications.[4] Its emphasis on actionable exemplars, implementation resources, and meaningful assessment supports AiR’s decision to treat completed work and reflection as the clearest evidence of learning.

The American Institutes for Research **AI by 8** initiative demonstrates that foundational AI literacy can be introduced in K–2 through storytelling, literacy-aligned lessons, playful inquiry, and unplugged activities that explain how machines “see,” “learn,” and “reason” without requiring children to begin on a screen.[5] This supports an AiR lesson pattern that starts with a concrete story or physical analogy before introducing a tool or technical term.

UNICEF’s **Guidance on AI and Children** identifies child-centered requirements including safety, privacy, fairness, transparency, accountability, well-being, inclusion, and preparing children for current and future AI developments.[6] AiR will therefore use privacy-minimizing profiles, safe display names, no public age field, no direct messaging, structured posting prompts, explicit reporting affordances, and adult-admin moderation controls.

| External principle | AiR translation |
|---|---|
| Human-centred mindset | Begin with a person, problem, and desired human outcome before selecting a tool |
| Ethics and responsible citizenship | Identify who could be harmed, excluded, misrepresented, or exposed by the work |
| Understand → Apply → Create | Let learners enter each lesson through Explore, Create, or Build modes |
| Critical judgment | Treat every AI output as a draft requiring inspection, evidence, and a human decision |
| Risk proportionality | Increase verification and human control as consequences increase |
| Inclusion and sustainability | Make accessibility, audience fit, and appropriate non-use part of the core workflow |
| Functional literacy | Explain enough of how AI works for learners to set realistic expectations and recognize failure modes |
| Rhetorical literacy | Teach prompts as briefs shaped by audience, context, constraints, and desired action |
| Pedagogical literacy | Help trainers turn AI use into guided inquiry rather than answer delivery |
| Durable skills and attitudes | Reward curiosity, judgment, revision, responsibility, and follow-through rather than tool memorization |
| Storytelling and unplugged learning | Introduce each complex idea through a familiar story, object, game, or conversation before using a screen |
| Child-centered privacy | Collect only the information required for accounts and progress; keep sensitive profile details out of public community views |
| Child well-being and agency | Let learners pause, choose an entry mode, ask for adult help, and understand what the platform stores or shares |
